# DEFEND THE UNKNOWN — My Game Vision

> **Stage:** Design concept, not a feature-complete game or committed release scope  
> **Started:** October 26, 2026  
> **Author:** Dennis Hilk  
> **Deutsch:** [Mein ausführliches Spielkonzept](SPIELKONZEPT_DE.md)

## Why I want to build it

I made my first little 3D worlds with Conitec's 3D GameStudio around 1999. Later I experimented with Unreal Engine, including a military base with lights and motion detectors. Now I want to rediscover that feeling with Unreal Engine 5 and AI-assisted development.

Games like *Jane's AH-64D Longbow*, *Command & Conquer: Red Alert*, *C&C: Renegade*, *Duke Nukem 3D*, *World of Warcraft*, *Alone in the Dark*, *Another World*, *The Secret of Monkey Island*, and *Zelda* shaped my taste. My goal isn't a remake. I want the systems, tension, exploration, and attachment I enjoyed to come together in something of my own.

## The premise

I arrive as the newly appointed commander of an old desert military outpost. Three soldiers — also newly assigned — arrived just before me. None of us knows what happened to the previous personnel.

I restore the base, build defenses, issue orders, explore on foot, and sometimes travel beyond the perimeter. Initially, our adversaries seem human: armed raiders, contractors, saboteurs, or unidentified groups. Subtle anomalies gradually suggest a larger mystery.

**The base is a place I inhabit, not a top-down icon.** I can walk into the command center, climb a watchtower, visit the garage, inspect my own equipment, and speak to the people keeping everything running. A tactical view makes planning and building comfortable without replacing that first-person perspective.

## How I want the game to begin

I want time to look around **before starting the real operation**. I can learn controls, prepare equipment, train, and spend my limited starting materials, but I cannot scout the entire map while enemies are inactive. A command-center action — or crossing the secure perimeter after a clear warning — starts the campaign activity.

My first mission has a purpose: HQ sends me to a nearby supply post that has gone silent. On the way I find a bullet-riddled military vehicle, with no survivors and no bodies. I report it, optionally recover useful material, and continue. The supply post itself is strangely intact but deserted. I secure supplies and take them home, myself or through an assigned transport team. Back at base, those materials introduce basic construction. HQ acknowledges the missing personnel and tells me to focus on securing the outpost.

This is a gameplay tutorial **inside a believable mission**, not an avalanche of pop-ups. The supply post stays unoccupied until I eventually have the people and resources to secure it.

## Systems that make the outpost feel real

### People, roles, and routines

I want soldiers with names, ranks, individual skills, equipment, a bit of personality, and history. Mechanics are good at repairs; trained drivers handle vehicles; pilots fly aircraft. A mechanic can still carry a rifle, but that doesn't make him an expert marksman. People learn from experience, and experienced soldiers can teach others.

My personnel should follow sensible routines — watch duty, meals, sleep, maintenance, conversations, training — without requiring me to manage every minute. On alarm, military personnel move to assigned combat positions. When the all-clear is given, they resume unfinished duties unless ordered otherwise. If engineers are minutes from completing a critical tower, they can ask whether to finish it or join the defense. **That question does not magically stop the battle.**

I can form squads (Alpha, Bravo, Charlie), appoint leaders, define fire discipline, ask for an entire group to report, and delegate command to a capable deputy. The deputy handles ordinary decisions and summarizes them in a digital duty log. I only want to be interrupted when my judgment is actually needed.

### Tactical decisions and imperfect knowledge

Enemy forces can scout, attack, retreat, surrender, or change approaches based on **what they have observed**. They should not know about a weapon position they never discovered. Guard posts, cameras, radar, motion sensors, and reconnaissance contribute real but limited information.

From a bunker I may only have radio contact with the surface. A soldier who is wounded may not answer my call. I must then make decisions with incomplete reports, not access an omniscient map.

A sniper with a spotter gains better target information but may be easier to notice; alone he is stealthier. As the spotter I can mark people with binoculars and authorize engagement. My soldiers may warn me that a plan looks disastrous, but if I insist, they follow my orders and I live with the consequences.

### Build, repair, and salvage

I want freely placed buildings, sensible site planning, visible construction, partially recoverable materials on demolition, and roads ranging from dirt tracks to expensive asphalt. I am deliberately not looking for a compulsory construction-economy simulator.

Damaged vehicles can be towed home, inspected, repaired, stripped for parts, or scrapped. A mechanic's assessment gives **estimated** recoverability, not perfect certainty. Finding compatible parts saves time; fabricating or improvising replacements can be slower. Skill with one vehicle family does not automatically translate to every other model.

Convoys use real soldiers and vehicles. Sending both mechanics away means there are no mechanics at home until they return. Radio recalls are allowed, but distance and travel time still count.

### Research and surveillance

New technology should often be **found or salvaged first**. I can assign specialists to analysis, choose careful or risky methods, build a prototype, test it, and only then manufacture more if materials permit. Risky analysis can destroy the specimen while yielding a little scrap.

A small motion sensor is subtle but tells me little. A camera can show and record more, yet is visible and limited by its angle. Some cameras pan; some work better at night; no single device does everything. Someone must watch the screens if I want consistent human monitoring. Early remote sensors use long-life batteries; later upgrades may add solar power. Drones need power or fuel, communications, and trained operators.

### HQ, visitors, and an inhabited world

My standing with HQ grows through successful missions, but the commander remains commander — I don't want an arbitrary rank-grind. Better standing might mean access to personnel, supplies, or specialist support. HQ may be busy, unreachable, or refuse requests. I can accept, delay, or decline optional assignments depending on what my base can safely handle.

The desert has occasional traders, civilians, and small settlements, but it must not turn into a city-life simulator. I can bargain, barter, place uncertain orders, and build relationships with particular merchants. A trader may have useless pipes one day and a rare radio the next. I can help him when his truck breaks down — if I can spare the mechanic.

One apparently ordinary man might be observed watching the base from afar, disappearing if approached. If the same-looking man later visits as a trader, the player may recognize him. Characters who never saw him before won't. He may simply say he was curious about the old base. If I never speak to him, the wider story continues. **I don't want a plot that requires clicking the one correct NPC.**

### Air support and personnel recovery

HQ may temporarily send a **Huey-like utility helicopter** for transport or medevac. It takes time to arrive and is not always available. Later I may operate more helipads. An **Apache-like attack helicopter** serves a different role: combat, not squad transport. I might eventually station one, but it needs personnel, upkeep, and facilities.

My commander isn't automatically qualified to pilot a helicopter. With instruction, he may be able to operate weapon systems from the copilot/gunner position. Crash-landed aircraft and disabled vehicles can create optional recovery missions.

Lightly wounded personnel can recover in an early small infirmary; serious cases may require evacuation and eventually a larger hospital. The campaign is intended to offer an optional permanent-death setting for personnel; when disabled, they recover and return after an absence. If **my commander** is killed in action, the consequence is game over and loading my last save — giving personal deployments genuine risk.

## The mystery beneath the outpost

During demolition or construction, my engineers may hit an uncharted structure below the base. I can assign workers to uncover it or postpone the work. Ultimately it leads to a **welded-shut bunker entrance**.

I decide whether to report the discovery. If I do, HQ's response is only that the facility is **not part of my current assignment**.

The underground complex is dark and initially powerless. I can explore alone or with a squad, hear something bang behind a locked door, and bring in specialists to restore parts of the installation. Old surveillance footage may be incomplete precisely where it matters. The mystery should unfold slowly, through tangible systems and discoveries, while the base above **continues living and facing threats**.

## Player choice without excessive busywork

I want two distinct experiences:

| Experience | Intent |
| --- | --- |
| **Campaign** | Limited resources, an unhurried initial preparation window, then an active world. No switching back to unlimited peaceful construction after the campaign starts. |
| **Only Build / Sandbox** | A separate save for peaceful construction, with optional defense tests that restore the pre-test state. |

I want to pause/save at any time, select time speed, and tune fuel and ammunition consumption (normal / reduced / off). I also like the idea of choosing **arcade or simulation-style vehicle controls** before play. These are design intentions, not completed settings.

## Reality check: the first prototype is far smaller

All of the above is an **aspirational design archive**. I want to test one small desert base, a handful of named soldiers, basic commands, one constructible defense, one simple jeep, and a readable hostile encounter first. If that loop isn't fun, a thousand extra features won't save it.

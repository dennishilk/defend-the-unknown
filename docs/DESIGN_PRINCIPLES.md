# Design Principles — My Decision Filter

> **DEFEND THE UNKNOWN** · Dennis Hilk · Concept-stage guidelines

These are the principles I want to keep in mind whenever a feature sounds exciting. They help me choose **what makes the game feel right**, rather than simply growing a list.

## 1. I am the commander, not the janitor

Personnel should perform their day-to-day jobs, keep records, check damage, follow routines, and use initiative **within their assignments**. I want to set goals and priorities, not micromanage every screw, guard shift, meal, or purchase.

**Example:** I order “keep enough 7.62 mm ammunition in stock.” The responsible specialist monitors inventory and sources materials. If supplies become critical, my deputy explains the shortage and suggests a decision.

## 2. Competence isn't omniscience

Soldiers, enemies, cameras, and HQ can only act on information they reasonably have. A soldier on a watchtower may identify movement; a cheap sensor may only report an activation. Someone underground with a working radio is not entitled to a bird's-eye view of events above.

**Example:** If I call Harris and receive silence, I do not automatically know whether he is wounded or the radio has failed.

## 3. Time, distance, and staffing must have consequences

I can send both mechanics away, but then none remain to repair my gate. I can call a convoy home, but it still has to drive home. I can salvage a vehicle, but someone must recover and examine it.

**Example:** A recovered tank may have a usable transmission, but confirming this and extracting it should cost time and involve uncertainty.

## 4. I want to choose my level of simulation

Realism is enjoyable when it creates interesting decisions, not repetitive chores. I want configurable fuel and ammunition rules, arcade/simulation-style vehicle controls, and a separate peaceful build sandbox. The campaign has finite resources and does not allow unlimited toggling back into a safe build state after the operation begins.

**Example:** A player can opt out of frequent refueling without turning the campaign into an unrestricted resource cheat by default.

## 5. The base should be a believable physical place

Buildings need space, access and construction time. Personnel work in and around them. Roads matter. I can walk through the command center, inspect a garage, climb a watchtower, and look back at my base from a distant checkpoint camera.

**Example:** If I build a hangar where the second gate should have gone, that's a real planning decision; the game should not silently teleport the hangar elsewhere.

## 6. Progress through doing, finding, and understanding

I prefer discovering a broken sensor, analyzing it, attempting a prototype, testing it, and then replicating it over unlocking everything from an abstract tech tree. More experience should make specialists faster and more reliable, not magically omnipotent.

**Example:** A technician may destroy a valuable component during a risky analysis and only recover scrap.

## 7. Mystery is patient

The narrative should offer odd observations and optional connections, not continually reveal itself through intrusive quest markers. I want room for uncertainty. Some characters may know details but never become mandatory story dispensers.

**Example:** If I ignore a peculiar trader, I might never discover who he was. The world and campaign can still progress.

## 8. The opening respects the player's time

I want a safe but **resource-limited** preparation window, followed by a clearly signposted operation start. Once I activate the campaign, I should receive a useful first assignment. Pause and save should remain available.

**Example:** My first mission leads to a silent supply station, useful materials, and an organic construction introduction.

## 9. AI-assisted development must be verifiable

I want to investigate what Codex and Unreal editor integrations can actually do, rather than crediting AI with work that still required human assembly. Claims need working builds, logs, screenshots, or reproducible tests.

**Example:** “Codex successfully changed a level and I verified the result” is useful evidence. “AI created the whole game” is not an acceptable claim without proof.

## 10. My first prototype stays small

This is a dream worth exploring, not a promise to build everything at once. I want to prove the **walking → commanding → building → defending** loop before adding vast worlds, helicopters, story systems, or complex economics.
